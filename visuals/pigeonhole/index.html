Pigeonhole: Guarantees, not Odds

Objective. Turn a probabilistic-sounding question into a certain one by constructing the worst case.
1.00x

Static explanation

The pigeonhole principle answers "must this happen", not "is it likely". If N objects sit in k boxes, some box holds at least ceil(N/k). To force r objects into some one box you need k(r - 1) + 1 objects.

For the classic card question: 4 suits, force 3 of one suit, so 4 x (3 - 1) + 1 = 9 cards. The proof is the worst case - put 2 cards in each suit, which uses 8 cards and still fails the claim; the next card cannot avoid creating a three.

The exam-safe procedure is to negate the claim and try to realise the negation. If the negation can be built, the claim is false. If it cannot, the claim is guaranteed.

Useful negations: not(x >= n) is x <= n - 1, and not(x <= n) is x >= n + 1.

Common misconception

“At least” and “exactly” are different guarantees. Nine cards guarantee that some suit has at least three, but they do not guarantee any suit has exactly three - the split could be 4, 2, 2, 1. Reading “exactly” into a pigeonhole conclusion is the most common lost mark in this topic.

Quick check

How many people must be in a room to guarantee two share a birth month?

Answer

13. Twelve months are the boxes; 12 people could all be born in different months, and the 13th must repeat one.
