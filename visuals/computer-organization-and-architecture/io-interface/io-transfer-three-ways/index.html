One Block, Three Ways: Programmed I/O, Interrupts and DMA on the Same Scale

Objective. Move the same 500 bytes from a device into memory three times and watch what the CPU is doing during the transfer. In programmed I/O it is in a status loop for the whole 100,000 cycles. In interrupt-driven I/O it is free between bytes but pays a 7-cycle service routine for every one, 3502 cycles in all. In DMA it programmes three registers in 20 cycles, leaves the bus, and is interrupted once at the end, 1020 cycles in all. The three numbers are what every GATE question in this topic is built on.
1.00x

Static explanation

Take 500 bytes and a device that produces one byte every 200 cycles. The transfer therefore spans 500 x 200 = 100,000 cycles of device time, and no transfer scheme can shorten that: the device is the slow party. What the schemes change is the CPU's share of those 100,000 cycles.

Programmed I/O puts the CPU inside a status loop for the whole span, so its share is 100,000 cycles and almost all of it is waiting. Interrupt-driven I/O removes the waiting: the interface watches the device and interrupts when a byte is ready, so the CPU is free between bytes. Its share is 2 + 500 x 7 = 3502 cycles, because the service routine still loads the byte, stores it, increments the address, decrements the count and branches. DMA removes the per-byte work: the CPU writes the start address, the count of 500 and a control word - 20 cycles - and then leaves the bus, so its share is 20 cycles plus one completion interrupt, with 1000 cycles of bus time (2 per byte) taken from the CPU by the controller.

The comparison the exam asks for is therefore 100,000 against 3502 against 1020, and the speed-up of DMA over the interrupt routine is 3502 / 1020 = 3.43, the 3.4 of GATE CSE 2011 Q.28. The two DMA modes differ only in how the 1000 cycles of bus time are shared: cycle stealing takes one byte's worth at a time and hands the bus back, so the CPU is slowed but keeps running; burst holds the bus for the whole block, so throughput is highest and the CPU is stalled throughout.

Common misconception

Two errors dominate this topic. The first is to think DMA means the CPU is idle: it is not idle, it is running other work, and it is still interrupted once per block - the transfer is not free, it is merely out of the CPU's instruction stream. The second is to read cycle stealing as stopping the CPU: one cycle is stolen, then the bus is handed back, so the CPU loses a fraction of its cycles rather than the whole block. Burst is the mode that stalls the CPU for the block, and it is the one with the higher throughput.

Quick check

The same 500-byte block, but the service routine is optimised to 5 cycles per byte and the DMA controller still needs 20 cycles of setup and 2 per byte. What is the speed-up now, and what does the change tell you?

Answer

2 + 500 x 5 = 2502 cycles against 1020, a speed-up of 2.45. The speed-up falls because DMA's advantage is the per-byte work the CPU no longer does: the cheaper the service routine becomes, the less there is for DMA to remove, while the 20-cycle setup stays fixed. It is the reason DMA pays off for blocks and not for single bytes.
