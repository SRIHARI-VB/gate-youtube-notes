Where L Comes From: Elimination Recording Its Own Multipliers

Objective. Watch one 3 by 3 matrix being eliminated, and see that the multiplier used at each step is exactly the entry that lands in L. By the end, L and U are both on the page, the product checks entry by entry, and the determinant falls out of the pivots. The last step shows the case where a zero pivot forces a row swap, and the factorization becomes PA = LU.
1.00x

Static explanation

Elimination turns a matrix into an upper triangular one by subtracting multiples of a row from the rows below it. Each such step is described by one number: the multiplier, which is the entry being cleared divided by the pivot above it. The claim behind LU decomposition is that those multipliers, written into the positions they cleared, form a lower triangular matrix L, and the triangular matrix elimination produced is U, with A equal to their product.

For A = [[2,3,1],[4,7,7],[-2,4,5]] the first column is cleared with the multipliers 4/2 = 2 and -2/2 = -1, giving [[2,3,1],[0,1,5],[0,7,6]]. The second column is then cleared with the multiplier 7/1 = 7, and the last entry becomes 6 - 7*5 = -29. So L = [[1,0,0],[2,1,0],[-1,7,1]] and U = [[2,3,1],[0,1,5],[0,0,-29]]. Multiplying them back reproduces A, and any single entry can be checked on its own: entry (3,2) of A is row 3 of L times column 2 of U, (-1)(3) + (7)(1) + (1)(0) = 4.

The determinant needs no separate work. A triangular determinant is the product of its diagonal, L has determinant 1 because its diagonal is all 1s, so det(A) = det(U) = 2 * 1 * (-29) = -58, which is what the direct computation gives. When a row swap is needed the same identity holds with one correction: det(A) = det(U) / det(P), and det(P) is -1 for an odd number of swaps. That is the case in the last step, where A = [[0,2],[1,3]] has a zero in the pivot position, and the swap turns the problem into an already-triangular one.

Common misconception

Computing the multiplier from the original matrix instead of the working matrix. In the second column the entry being cleared is 7 and the pivot is 1, both taken after column 1 has been cleared; using A's original entries, 4 and 3, would give the wrong multiplier and a product that no longer equals A. The second mistake is a sign: adding a multiple of a row corresponds to a negative multiplier, so the operation R3 <- R3 + R1 records l31 = -1, not +1.

Quick check

A 3 by 3 matrix has l21 = 0 and l31 = 0. What did the elimination look like, and what does that say about the first column of A?

Answer

No row operation was needed in column 1, which happens exactly when the first entry of rows 2 and 3 is already 0. It says nothing about the rest of those rows and nothing about whether A is singular - a zero multiplier is the record of a step that was skipped, not of an entry that vanished.
