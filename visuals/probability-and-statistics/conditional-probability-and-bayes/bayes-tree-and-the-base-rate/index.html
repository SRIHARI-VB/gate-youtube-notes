Bayes on a Tree and the Base Rate

Objective. Read one Bayes question as a tree - causes on the first level, evidence on the second - and see that the answer is one branch's share of the branches that end in the evidence. Then watch the same likelihoods give a different answer when only the priors change.
1.00x

Static explanation

A factory receives 60 per cent of its items from machine A and 40 per cent from machine B. Machine A makes 2 per cent defective items and machine B makes 5 per cent. An item is drawn and found defective; the question asks which machine made it. That is a Bayes question, and it is a tree question.

The first level of the tree is the cause, which we do not observe: 0.6 and 0.4, summing to 1 because every item comes from exactly one machine. The second level is the evidence given the cause: 0.02 and 0.05, the two likelihoods. A branch's probability is the product along it, so machine A and defective is 0.6 times 0.02, which is 0.012, and machine B and defective is 0.4 times 0.05, which is 0.020.

The denominator is the total probability of the evidence: 0.012 + 0.020 = 0.032. Those are the only branches that end in the observation, so they are the only ones in the sum. Dividing, the defective item came from machine B with probability 0.020 / 0.032 = 5/8 = 0.625, and from machine A with probability 0.375. The two shares add to 1, which is the check to run on every Bayes answer.

The last step changes only the priors: machine B now supplies 90 per cent of the output. The branches become 0.002 and 0.045, the evidence 0.047, and the posterior for B 0.957. The machines did not improve. The population did. That is why a likelihood on its own can never answer a question about causes, and why the base rate has to be in the calculation.

Common misconception

Reporting 0.05, the defect rate of machine B, as the answer. That is P(defective | B), a property of the machine; the question asks for P(B | defective), a share of the defectives. The two numbers are 0.05 and 0.625 on the same data. The second mistake is adding all four branch products in the denominator, which sums to 1 and turns the answer back into the joint probability 0.012 or 0.020.

Quick check

With the original 60/40 split, what is P(A | defective), and what does it add to with P(B | defective)?

Answer

0.012 / 0.032 = 0.375, and 0.375 + 0.625 = 1. The posterior probabilities over a partition always sum to 1, because every defective item came from one of the two machines.
