Cyclic Subgroups and Lagrange's Theorem in Z12

Objective. Watch one element generate a subgroup, watch the cosets of that subgroup tile the group, and see why every subgroup order must divide the group order - the counting argument behind GATE's "largest possible proper subgroup" questions.
1.00x

Static explanation

Z12 is the set {0, 1, ..., 11} with addition modulo 12. It is a group of order 12: the identity is 0 and the inverse of a is 12 - a. Start at 0 and keep adding 4. The walk goes 0, 4, 8 and then back to 0, because 8 + 4 = 12 is 0 modulo 12. The three residues reached form the cyclic subgroup <4>, and the number of steps needed to return to the identity is the order of the element 4, which is 3.

A subgroup of order 3 inside a group of order 12 leaves room for exactly 12 / 3 = 4 shifted copies: {0, 4, 8}, {1, 5, 9}, {2, 6, 10} and {3, 7, 11}. Each copy has three elements, the copies never overlap, and together they cover all twelve residues. That is the coset partition, and it is the entire proof of Lagrange's theorem: the group is a union of equal-sized blocks, so the block size divides the group size.

The element 5 behaves differently. Its multiples are 5, 10, 3, 8, 1, 6, 11, 4, 9, 2, 7 and only then 0, so it reaches every residue and generates the whole group. The generators of Z12 are the residues coprime to 12 - namely 1, 5, 7 and 11 - and there are phi(12) = 4 of them. An element whose order equals the group order always generates the group, and that is what "cyclic" means.

The last step collects the subgroups of Z12 by order: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 12. There are exactly six, one per divisor of 12, and no others. The largest proper one has order 6. Contrast this with a group of order 35: its divisors are 1, 5, 7 and 35, so the largest proper subgroup has order 7, not 35 / 2. That contrast is what GATE CSE 2018 Q.19 and 2020 Q.18 test.

Common misconception

Reading Lagrange backwards. The theorem says a subgroup order must divide the group order; it does not say every divisor is realised. Z12 is the friendly case, because cyclic groups do realise every divisor, which is exactly why this example can mislead. In the alternating group A4, of order 12, there is no subgroup of order 6 at all, even though 6 divides 12. The second half of the misconception is arithmetic: "largest proper subgroup" is |G| divided by the smallest prime factor of |G|, which gives 42 for 84, 7 for 35 and 15 for 45 - never |G| / 2 unless 2 divides |G|.

Quick check

In Z12, how many elements generate the group, and what is the index of the subgroup <4>?

Answer

Four generators (1, 5, 7, 11) and index 4. A residue a generates Z12 exactly when gcd(a, 12) = 1, and phi(12) = 4 counts them. The subgroup <4> has order 3, so it has 12 / 3 = 4 cosets, and the index is 4 - the same number, for a different reason: the index is the number of blocks, the generator count is the number of coprime residues.
