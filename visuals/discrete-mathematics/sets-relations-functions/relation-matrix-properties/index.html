One Grid, Five Properties: Reading a Relation as a Matrix

Objective. Read reflexivity, symmetry, antisymmetry, transitivity and equivalence straight off a 3 by 3 grid, and see where the counting formulas 2 to the 9, 2 to the 6, 2 to the 6 and 2 cubed times 3 cubed come from.
1.00x

Static explanation

A relation on the set {1, 2, 3} is a subset of the nine ordered pairs. Draw a 3 by 3 grid with rows indexed by the first coordinate and columns by the second: a tick at row a, column b means the pair (a, b) belongs to the relation. Because each of the nine cells is either ticked or not, there are 2 to the 9 = 512 relations in total.

Each property is a constraint on that grid. Reflexive forces the three diagonal cells to be ticked, leaving the six off-diagonal cells free: 2 to the 6 = 64. Irreflexive forces the diagonal to be empty, which is also 2 to the 6 = 64, and no relation is both unless the set is empty. Symmetric makes each mirror pair of off-diagonal cells all-or-nothing, so the three diagonal cells and the three pairs give six free decisions: 2 to the 6 = 64. Antisymmetric forbids ticking both cells of a mirror pair, so each of the three pairs has three states (neither, one way, the other way) and the diagonal is free: 2 cubed times 3 cubed = 216.

Transitive is the only property that looks at three cells at once: if (a, b) and (b, c) are ticked then (a, c) must be ticked. On the grid, a tick at (1, 2) and a tick at (2, 3) force a tick at (1, 3). Adding every forced tick, and repeating, gives the transitive closure; Warshall's algorithm computes it in cubic time by allowing one intermediate vertex at a time.

An equivalence relation is reflexive, symmetric and transitive together, and then the ticks form square blocks along the diagonal. Each block is one equivalence class, the blocks partition the set, and the number of equivalence relations on an n-element set is the number of partitions of it: 5 for three elements and 15 for four.

Common misconception

Reading antisymmetric as "not symmetric". The identity relation is both symmetric and antisymmetric at once, so neither property excludes the other. On {1, 2} the relation {(1,1), (1,2)} is antisymmetric and not symmetric. The relation {(1,2)} shows the neighbouring trap: it is irreflexive rather than reflexive, and reflexive plus symmetric is a separate condition again. Symmetric plus antisymmetric is exactly a subset of the identity relation, so there are 2 to the n of those on an n-element set, not zero.

Quick check

On a 4-element set, how many relations are symmetric, how many are reflexive, and how many are symmetric and antisymmetric?

Answer

1024 symmetric, 4096 reflexive, 16 symmetric and antisymmetric. Symmetric: the 4 diagonal cells plus the 6 mirror pairs are free decisions, so 2 to the 10 = 1024. Reflexive: the diagonal is forced and the 12 off-diagonal cells are free, so 2 to the 12 = 4096. Symmetric and antisymmetric: the relation is a subset of the identity, so 2 to the 4 = 16.
