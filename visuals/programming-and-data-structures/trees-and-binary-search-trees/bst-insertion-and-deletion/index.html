Insert at a Leaf, Delete by Replacement

Objective. Insert 10, -4, 15, 30, 20, 5, 60, 19 in that order and watch 19 land four edges below the root - the answer to GATE CSE 2025 Set 2 Q.25. Then delete a node with two children and see why the inorder successor, not a child, is what replaces it.
1.00x

Static explanation

Insertion is a search that fails. The new key is compared with the root, then with the next node on the chosen side, and so on until the comparison would step into an empty slot; the new node is hung there. A new key is therefore always a leaf, and existing keys never move. Because the path followed is the search path, the depth at which a key lands is fixed by the keys inserted before it - which is the whole content of the 2025 question.

The order 10, -4, 15, 30, 20, 5, 60, 19 gives 10 at the root; -4 and 15 as its children; 30 to the right of 15; 20 to the left of 30; 5 to the right of -4; 60 to the right of 30; and 19 to the left of 20. The path to 19 is 10, 15, 30, 20, 19: five nodes and four edges. Counting nodes instead of edges is the mistake the question is built to catch.

Deletion has three cases. A leaf is unlinked. A node with one child has that child spliced into its place, which is safe because the child's whole subtree lies on the same side of every ancestor as the deleted node did. A node with two children cannot be spliced at all, because two subtrees would have to occupy one slot, so its key is overwritten with its inorder successor - the minimum of its right subtree - and the node that held that successor is then deleted. That node has no left child, so its own deletion is one of the two easy cases.

Here the node 30 has children 20 and 60. The minimum of its right subtree is 60, so 30's key becomes 60 and the node that held 60 is removed. The result is a single key write and a single pointer change, and the inorder 19, 20, 60 is still sorted, so the tree is still a search tree.

Common misconception

That a two-child node is deleted by promoting one of its children. Promotion overwrites a pointer, so if the promoted child already has a subtree on that side, that subtree is silently lost and the tree no longer holds the same keys. The replacement must be the inorder successor or the inorder predecessor, and both are guaranteed to have at most one child.

Quick check

In the final tree, delete 20. Which key replaces it?

Answer

None: 20 has only a left child, 19, so 19 is spliced into 20's place. Only the two-child case replaces a key. Splicing works because 19 and everything under it is smaller than 60 and larger than 15, exactly as 20 was, so no ancestor's ordering is disturbed.
